Who should get the e-mail?

A randomised test of two marketing e-mails on 64,000 customers of an online retailer. First the A/B analysis: did each e-mail work, and by how much? Then uplift models that predict whom each e-mail persuades, and an off-policy evaluation of targeting rules.

Can the experiment be trusted?

Customers were randomly split three ways for two weeks: a men’s merchandise e-mail, a women’s merchandise e-mail, or no e-mail. Before reading any result, check that randomisation worked: the arms should be the planned size and look alike before treatment.

Does the revenue result hold up?

Revenue is the outcome the business cares about and the hardest to measure: about 99% of customers spend nothing in two weeks, and a few spend hundreds. Three different interval methods are compared below.

Extra revenue per customer, three ways

95% intervals: Welch t-test, bootstrap (4,000 resamples), regression-adjusted

Variance reduction from covariates

Lin (2013) regression adjustment with HC2 standard errors

Past spend, recency, channel and area barely predict who buys in a two-week window, so adjusting for them narrows the intervals by under 2%. The unadjusted estimates stand.

Can a model find the persuadable customers?

Uplift models predict, for each customer, how much an e-mail changes their chance of visiting. Four learners were trained on 70% of customers; the one with the best cross-validated AUUC was kept, and everything shown is scored on the untouched 30%.

Methods

Data. Kevin Hillstrom’s MineThatData e-mail challenge (2008): 64,000 customers who bought in the past year, randomly assigned to a men’s e-mail, a women’s e-mail or no e-mail, with visits, conversions and spend tracked for two weeks. The download is checked against a SHA-256 hash.

Randomisation checks. A chi-square sample-ratio-mismatch test on arm sizes and standardised mean differences for every pre-treatment covariate.

Average effects. Two-proportion z-tests with Wald intervals for rates, Welch’s t-test for revenue, delta-method intervals for relative lift, and Holm correction across the six comparisons. Revenue is cross-checked with a percentile bootstrap and Lin’s (2013) fully interacted regression adjustment with HC2 standard errors.

Heterogeneity. The same tests within segments defined by pre-treatment traits. These are exploratory; the uplift models below are the principled version of the same question.

Uplift models. T-learner and S-learner (histogram gradient boosting), the class-transformation method, and a T-learner with logistic regression, all predicting the change in visit probability. Conversions (about 1%) are too sparse to model reliably.

Evaluation. A stratified 70/30 customer split. The model is chosen by five-fold cross-validated AUUC on the 70%; the uplift curve, deciles and a bootstrap interval for AUUC are computed on the untouched 30%.

Policy evaluation. Per-arm response models choose an e-mail (or none) per customer. Each policy is valued on the hold-out by inverse-propensity weighting, with paired bootstrap intervals for the difference from sending the men’s e-mail to everyone.

Limitations. One two-week campaign from 2008, so long-term effects (unsubscribes, repeat purchases) are unobserved. The simulator’s value per visit is an average (incremental revenue ÷ incremental visits), not a per-customer estimate.
